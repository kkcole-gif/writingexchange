01

How to Use the Crosswalk

Each entry maps an administrative term or concern to a writing studies frame. The "Say this" column offers language to adapt for meetings, email, program proposals, and accreditation documents. Treat it as a starting point and revise it for your institution.

Suggested uses

  • Before a budget or accreditation meeting: filter by theme and review the "Say this" column.
  • Designing faculty development: draw concepts from the "Writing studies frame" column.
  • Onboarding new WPAs: teach from the table alongside the cited position statements.
  • Advocacy writing and grant proposals: draw citable sources from the "Key sources" column.
  • When a dean says "student success" and means "graduation rate": start with the first row.

Primary audiences

  • WPAs and WAC or WID directors preparing for conversations with deans, provosts, or accrediting bodies
  • New faculty and early-career administrators learning the translation work of the field
  • Department chairs and deans seeking the disciplinary rationale behind program decisions

1Student Success and High-Impact Learning

Student Success · Student Retention · Graduation Rate · Student Satisfaction

Administrative focusMetrics of student progression, completion, and academic achievement.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Writing as a high-impact practice AAC&U lists writing-intensive courses among its high-impact practices, and George Kuh's 2008 report for AAC&U associates those practices with student engagement and persistence. Writing courses belong to the institution's student success infrastructure. They are not curricular overhead.
  • AAC&U, High-Impact Practices (opens in a new tab)
  • Kuh, High-Impact Educational Practices (AAC&U, 2008)
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
Metacognition and transfer of learning When students reflect on their writing processes and apply what they learn across contexts, they build the transferable knowledge general education is designed to produce. Transfer is not automatic. A curriculum has to teach for it.
  • Framework for Success in Postsecondary Writing (CWPA, NCTE, NWP, 2011) (opens in a new tab)
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
  • NCTE, Professional Knowledge for the Teaching of Writing (2016) (opens in a new tab)
Ongoing, developmental writing growth Writing development is cumulative. One required course is a threshold and not a destination. Writing distributed across the curriculum gives students the sustained practice through which ability changes.
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
  • Framework for Success in Postsecondary Writing (CWPA, NCTE, NWP, 2011) (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)

Foundational Literacies

Administrative focusGeneral education. Functional reading, writing, and communication skills.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Rhetorical competence, genre awareness, and critical thinking through writing The administrative term "foundational literacies" corresponds to rhetorical competence in writing studies: the ability to read context, purpose, and audience and to communicate accordingly. The concept is more specific and more teachable than "communication skills."
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
  • NCTE, Professional Knowledge for the Teaching of Writing (2016) (opens in a new tab)
  • NCTE, Media Education in English Language Arts (2022) (opens in a new tab)

2Workforce Readiness and Communicative Competence

Workforce Readiness · Enhance Quality · Expand Reach and Impact · Competency-Based Education

Administrative focusEquip students with the skills employers demand. Enhance institutional reputation.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Rhetorical competence and rhetorical agility Workforce readiness in communication means rhetorical agility: adapting writing and speaking across audiences, disciplines, and professional contexts. Writing courses develop this capacity when they are well designed and adequately staffed.
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)
  • ATTW, Code of Ethics and Mission (opens in a new tab)
Multimodal communication Workplace communication is increasingly multimodal. Programs limited to print-based writing leave students underprepared for the visual, digital, and oral composing professional roles now require.
  • NCTE, Media Education in English Language Arts (2022) (opens in a new tab)
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)
Genre awareness and fluency Genre fluency, the knowledge of how memos, reports, proposals, and white papers work, is concrete and teachable. It gives a program a specific answer when employers ask for graduates who write well in professional contexts.
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
  • NCTE, Professional Knowledge for the Teaching of Writing (2016) (opens in a new tab)
Professional and technical communication Technical and professional communication is a specialization with its own research base, pedagogy, and ethics. ATTW's standards and the journal Technical Communication Quarterly guide programs in this area.
  • ATTW, Code of Ethics and Mission (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)

3Curriculum Reform, WAC, and Program Design

Curricular Reform · General Education Reform · Streamlining Services

Administrative focusEfficiency, modernization, and accreditation standards.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Writing Across the Curriculum and Writing in the Disciplines Writing Across the Curriculum has organized curricular reform in U.S. higher education since the 1970s. It distributes writing instruction across programs and builds on the expertise of disciplinary faculty. It requires sustained faculty development, and it does not succeed as an unfunded mandate.
  • AWAC, Statement on AI and Writing Across the Curriculum (v2.0, 2025) (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)
  • Cox, Galin, and Melzer, Sustainable WAC (2018)
  • Carter and Matzke (Eds.), Systems Shift (2023)
  • Perryman-Clark, The New Work of Writing Across the Curriculum (2023)
Programmatic coherence Coherent writing instruction is sequenced and scaffolded: students build from introductory to advanced work across the degree. Without coordination, writing requirements become checkboxes.
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
  • CWPA, Evaluating the Intellectual Work of Writing Administration (1998) (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)
  • Carter and Matzke (Eds.), Systems Shift (2023)
Pedagogical expertise in writing Teaching writing is a specialized practice, distinct from writing well. Faculty development in writing pedagogy is the mechanism through which WAC policy becomes classroom practice. Research on its effects relies mostly on self-report and program evaluation, so state claims about outcomes with care.
  • CCCC, Statement on Preparing Teachers of College Writing (2015) (opens in a new tab)
  • NCTE, Professional Knowledge for the Teaching of Writing (2016) (opens in a new tab)
  • TYCA, Guidelines for Preparing Teachers of English in the Two-Year College (2016) (opens in a new tab)
Rhetorical ecologies Writing develops within a whole learning environment: assignment design, feedback culture, physical space, and digital tools. Improving writing outcomes requires attention to this environment as well as to course requirements.
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)

4Technology, AI, and Operational Efficiency

Operational Efficiency

Administrative focusOptimizing processes, reducing labor costs, and using technology for perceived productivity gains.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Recursive writing process Efficiency in writing does not come from shortening revision. It comes from teaching reliable strategies for drafting and revising. Graham and Perin's 2007 meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction reports its largest effects for explicit strategy instruction.
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
  • NCTE, Professional Knowledge for the Teaching of Writing (2016) (opens in a new tab)
  • NCTE, Understanding and Teaching Writing: Guiding Principles (2018) (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)
  • Graham and Perin, Writing Next (2007)
Human agency and the "human in the loop" AI tools support writers, and they do not replace the learning writing produces. Keeping people substantively in the loop is the educational rationale for assigning writing.
  • MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI, Working Papers (2023 to 2024) (opens in a new tab)
  • AWAC, Statement on AI and Writing Across the Curriculum (v2.0, 2025) (opens in a new tab)
AI literacy AI literacy means students critically evaluate, appropriately use, and ethically engage with generative AI tools. Writing studies has the pedagogical infrastructure to teach it. Research on AI-mediated writing is still too young to show gains in transferable writing ability, so institutional claims should stay modest.
  • MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI, Working Papers (2023 to 2024) (opens in a new tab)
  • AWAC, Statement on AI and Writing Across the Curriculum (v2.0, 2025) (opens in a new tab)
  • AWAC, Statement on AI Writing Tools in WAC (2023) (opens in a new tab)
Labor equity The quality of writing instruction depends on instructor working conditions. Unsustainable workloads reduce the time available for response to student writing. Efficiency measures which hollow out instruction carry costs of their own.
  • CCCC, Statement on Working Conditions for Non-Tenure-Track Writing Faculty (2016) (opens in a new tab)
  • TYCA, White Paper on Two-Year College English Faculty Workload (2021) (opens in a new tab)
  • NC State Invisible Labor Study (opens in a new tab)

5Assessment, Accreditation, and Learning Outcomes

Accreditation · Quality Improvement

Administrative focusMeeting external standards. Systematic evaluation.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Programmatic assessment Accreditation-ready writing assessment rests on systematic evidence of student learning over time, gathered through portfolios or locally designed rubrics, and not on course grades alone. CCCC's assessment statement and AAC&U's Written Communication VALUE rubric give reviewers evidence in a form they recognize.
  • CCCC, Writing Assessment: A Position Statement (2022) (opens in a new tab)
  • AAC&U, VALUE Rubric for Written Communication (opens in a new tab)
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
Learning outcomes Writing outcomes should describe what students do with writing, and not only the forms they have practiced. The WPA Outcomes Statement is the field's benchmark for first-year writing, and the VALUE rubric offers one instrument for assessing it.
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)
  • AAC&U, VALUE Rubric for Written Communication (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Writing Assessment: A Position Statement (2022) (opens in a new tab)
Faculty development WAC faculty development connects disciplinary expertise with writing pedagogy. It is the mechanism through which WAC policy reaches classrooms, and it depends on chairs, deans, scheduling, and budget as well as on willing faculty.
  • CCCC, Statement on Preparing Teachers of College Writing (2015) (opens in a new tab)
  • TYCA, Guidelines for Preparing Teachers of English in the Two-Year College (2016) (opens in a new tab)
  • AWAC, Statement on AI and Writing Across the Curriculum (v2.0, 2025) (opens in a new tab)

6Faculty Labor, Roles, and Working Conditions

Employee Well-Being · Institutional Reputation

Administrative focusFaculty working conditions and institutional standing.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Class size CCCC's Principles set a maximum of 20 students per writing class, with 15 preferred, and no more than 60 writing students per instructor per term. These figures are professional norms grounded in workload analysis. Above them, the time an instructor has for each student's writing shrinks.
  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (2023) (opens in a new tab)
  • TYCA, White Paper on Two-Year College English Faculty Workload (2021) (opens in a new tab)
  • NC State Invisible Labor Study (opens in a new tab)
Faculty workload Writing instruction is labor-intensive in ways specific to the work: reading drafts, responding, and conferencing. The TYCA workload white paper documents this labor at two-year colleges through a national survey of 1,062 faculty.
  • TYCA, White Paper on Two-Year College English Faculty Workload (2021) (opens in a new tab)
  • TYCA position statements and workload survey (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Statement on Working Conditions for Non-Tenure-Track Writing Faculty (2016) (opens in a new tab)
  • Cole, Giordano, and Hassel, Faculty Guidebook (Routledge, 2023)
  • NC State Invisible Labor Study (opens in a new tab)

Faculty Roles and Responsibilities · Promotion and Tenure

Administrative focusTeaching, research, service, and administration. Criteria for evaluation.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
WPA intellectual work and administrative scholarship Writing program administration is intellectual work. It draws on disciplinary expertise and produces knowledge about pedagogy, assessment, and curriculum. CWPA's 1998 statement supports counting this work toward tenure and promotion. Classifying it as service misrepresents it.

7Engagement, Innovation, and Institutional Identity

Entrepreneurial Mindset · Innovation

Administrative focusFostering curiosity and initiative, often in engineering and business contexts.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Writing-to-learn and rhetorical inquiry The "entrepreneurial mindset" of engineering and business programs corresponds to writing-to-learn in writing studies: low-stakes, exploratory writing which builds curiosity and iterative problem-solving. A 2004 meta-analysis of 48 studies found a small positive average effect on achievement, so assignment design and prompts matter.
  • AWAC, Statement on AI and Writing Across the Curriculum (v2.0, 2025) (opens in a new tab)
  • CCCC, Statement on the Multiple Uses of Writing (opens in a new tab)
  • NCTE, Professional Knowledge for the Teaching of Writing (2016) (opens in a new tab)
  • Bangert-Drowns, Hurley, and Wilkinson, "The Effects of School-Based Writing-to-Learn Interventions on Academic Achievement" (2004)

Campus Partnerships · Experiential Learning

Administrative focusConnecting to local communities. Hands-on learning.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Service-learning, public writing, and community engagement Service-learning appears on AAC&U's list of high-impact practices, and public writing gives students real audiences. Both make partnership work visible to the community and to reviewers.
  • CCCC, Statement on Community-Engaged Scholarship and Pedagogy (2023) (opens in a new tab)
  • AAC&U, High-Impact Practices (opens in a new tab)

Professionalism · Professional Identity Formation

Administrative focusConduct aligned with institutional and professional norms. Socialization into a field.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Socialization, hidden curriculum, and discursive norms "Professionalism" in professional programs corresponds to socialization into discursive norms, which often operate as a hidden curriculum. Making those norms explicit and teachable is more equitable than assuming them. It also raises the question of whose norms count as professional.
  • CCCC, Students' Right to Their Own Language (1974, reaffirmed 2014) (opens in a new tab)

Imposter Syndrome

Administrative focusA term for a social and psychological pattern among students.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Internalized oppression "Imposter syndrome" often reflects structural inequities in whose language, knowledge, and ways of knowing count as legitimate. Naming it as internalized oppression moves the site of intervention from the individual to the institution.
  • CCCC, Statement on Language, Power, and Action (2022) (opens in a new tab)

Continuous Quality Improvement · Evidence-Based · Longitudinal

Administrative focusConstant monitoring and improvement of processes. Data-driven decisions. Continuity over time.

Writing studies frameSay thisKey sources
Iteration, temporality, and empirical writing research The field's version of continuous improvement is recursive process: drafting, feedback, and revision over time. "Evidence-based" in writing studies refers to an empirical research base published in journals such as Research in the Teaching of English, WPA: Writing Program Administration, and College Composition and Communication, in addition to institutional metrics.
  • WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition (v3.0, 2014) (opens in a new tab)

Scope and Citation

The crosswalk draws on writing studies broadly conceived: composition, rhetoric, WAC and WID, technical communication, and writing program administration. Some entries cite books and empirical studies alongside formal position statements.

CCCC statements are cited by title and date of adoption, with links to archived copies in the CCCC-L position statement repository (opens in a new tab). See the note on CCCC statements. For the evidence behind the field's claims, see Field Commitments.
