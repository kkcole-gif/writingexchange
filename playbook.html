Labor Advocacy Playbook

01

Before You Act: Know Your Position

Assess your institutional power, position type, and risk tolerance before any advocacy. Positions differ in the influence they carry and in the risk they bear. The question is how to act, not whether to act.

The Playbook turns the CCCC Statement of Professional Guidance for New Faculty Members into adaptable institutional action.

  • Position A

    Contingent, non-tenure-track, or adjunct

    Course leads, long-term adjuncts, visiting instructors, and non-tenure-track lecturers. Formal governance power is limited. Proximity to students and to the effects of policy gives this position credibility.

    Higher risk: protect yourself first

    Build a coalition before acting. Document everything. Seek tenured allies before raising an issue in public. Consider anonymous or collective action.

    Institutional influence: 2 of 5

  • Position B

    Tenure-track or early-career WPA

    Assistant professors, early-career WPAs, and faculty in a first administrative role. Pre-tenure vulnerability exists on some issues. The role also carries formal authority and disciplinary expertise.

    Moderate risk: act strategically

    You have formal standing to raise disciplinary issues. Cite professional standards to move the argument away from personal preference. Build faculty allies. Document administrative work as scholarship.

    Institutional influence: 3 of 5

  • Position C

    Tenured or experienced WPA

    Associate and full professors, established program directors, and WPAs with long service at one institution. This position carries the strongest formal protection and institutional credibility, and with them a responsibility to advocate for others.

    Lower risk: act with full authority

    Lead coalition-building. Bring issues to governance formally. Use the position to protect contingent colleagues. Draw on a national profile where one exists.

    Institutional influence: 5 of 5

Self-assessment questions before any action

  • How long have you been at this institution?
  • How secure is your position?
  • Who are your allies?
  • Which channels exist for raising concerns?
  • Who holds the authority to change this?
  • What is your risk tolerance, and what does inaction cost?
02

The Six-Step Advocacy Process

Select a step to open its full guidance: key questions, actionable moves, and relevant professional standards. The steps build on one another, and each also stands alone.

Advocacy begins with an honest reading of institutional context. The purpose is not delay. The purpose is action proportionate to your position and risk tolerance, aimed at the place where change is possible.

Key questions

  • What is your job security: tenure, a long-term contract, a year-to-year appointment, or adjunct status?
  • How long have you been at this institution, and how do the relevant decision-makers regard your work?
  • What is the worst consequence of raising this issue, and is it one you are prepared to accept?
  • What has been tried before on this issue, and what happened?
  • Which formal governance structures exist: a curriculum committee, a faculty senate, collective bargaining?
  • What are the institution's stated priorities this year, and how does your issue fit within them?

Actionable moves

  • Write a private memo laying out your position, power, and goals. It clarifies your thinking and records your intent.
  • Review your contract or the faculty handbook for relevant policies before raising the issue formally.
  • Ask two or three senior colleagues who have handled similar issues for informal advice.
  • Map the decision structure: who holds authority over this issue, and where does the formal authority sit?
  • Read the political moment. Accreditation cycles, new administrators, program reviews, and budget conversations create openings.

If you are in a higher-risk position

  • Identify a tenured ally willing to raise the issue with you or on your behalf, with full credit to you.
  • Consider anonymous or collective data collection before attaching your name in public.
  • Know your union contract or collective bargaining agreement, if one exists. It often provides formal channels and protections.

Advocacy on structural issues such as class size and workload rarely succeeds when one person acts alone. This step maps who is affected and builds the coalition needed for change.

Coalition-building differs from sharing complaints. The aim is to find people who face the same problem, who are willing to work toward a solution, and who help you reach decision-makers.

Key questions

  • Who else is directly affected: faculty, students, staff, coordinators, writing center directors?
  • Which spaces already exist for raising the issue: department meetings, program committees, faculty learning communities?
  • Are there administrative allies, such as a dean of students, institutional research staff, or a new administrator?
  • Which external pressure points exist: accrediting bodies, state systems, professional organizations?

Actionable moves

  • Hold informal conversations before organizing anything formal. Listen more than you speak at this stage.
  • Identify a core group of three to five people across roles and ranks who are motivated and well placed.
  • Map governance: which committees, bodies, or processes have jurisdiction over the issue?
  • Look for moments with built-in urgency, such as program reviews, curricular revisions, and accreditation self-studies.
  • Find out whether your institution has an ombudsperson, a faculty senate, or a union with jurisdiction.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Do not share private conversations without explicit permission.
  • Do not read sympathy as willingness to act in public. Ask people directly what they are willing to do.
  • Do not organize by email before you are ready to go formal. Email creates a record.

Administrative decisions often rest on incomplete data. Your task is a fuller account: the metrics administrators already track (enrollment, student credit hours, DFW rates, retention) together with a disciplinary reading of what those numbers mean.

Data feminism matters here. Ask what the data shows, who produced it, what it was designed to measure, and what it systematically leaves out (D'Ignazio and Klein, 2020).

Data sources to pursue

  • Institutional research or institutional effectiveness: class size distributions, DFW rates by section, enrollment by course type and modality.
  • Registrar: historical enrollment, section caps, and waitlists. Waitlists document demand.
  • Faculty surveys: workload, time spent per student per week, capacity for feedback.
  • Student surveys: perceived quality of feedback, writing center access, connection to the instructor.
  • National data: the national course cap dataset in the Data Guide, the CCCC Principles, and the TYCA workload white paper.

Data feminism checks

  • Before presenting data, state its limits: what it does not measure, and why.
  • Ask whether the data overrepresents some groups, such as full-time faculty over adjuncts or residential students over commuters.
  • Pair numbers with accounts from faculty and students, with explicit permission from each person.
  • Credit the people whose labor produced the data, and ask whether they are in the room when it is presented.

A local complaint becomes a disciplinary argument when it rests on the field's published standards. Statements from CCCC, CWPA, TYCA, and MLA record professional consensus, and citing them moves the argument away from individual preference.

Find the statement closest to your issue, quote it accurately, and set your local data beside it.

Core standards to know

  • CCCC, Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing (revised 2023): no more than 20 students per writing class, 15 preferred, and no more than 60 writing students per instructor per term.
  • National course cap dataset: among 417 institutions reporting a first-year composition cap, the median is 22. The two-year college median is 25. About 37 percent report a cap of 20 or lower.
  • CWPA, Evaluating the Intellectual Work of Writing Administration (1998): writing program administration is scholarship and merits evaluation as scholarship in tenure and promotion review.

How to use the standards in a meeting

  • Quote the standard exactly and bring a printed copy. Precision matters with administrators.
  • Set your data beside it: "Our cap of 27 sits 7 students above the CCCC maximum of 20 and 2 above the national two-year college median of 25."
  • Frame the issue as professional ethics: "This is the standard the field has set for effective instruction. It is not my preference."
  • If you meet resistance, return to the data: "Among 417 institutions reporting a first-year composition cap, the median is 22. Ours is 27."
  • Present the gap as a student success issue. Larger sections leave less time for response to each student's writing, and the CCCC Principles tie class size to the conditions for effective instruction.

A note on evidence

  • The 20 and 60 figures are professional norms grounded in workload analysis. Present them as the field's standard, and do not present them as an experimentally established threshold. Field Commitments explains the distinction.

With data, national context, and a coalition in place, you need an occasion for collective interpretation. A formal presentation is one option. A department retreat activity, a program committee discussion, and a faculty learning community meeting also serve.

The strategic question is whether to include administrative leadership from the start or to build consensus first.

Key strategic decisions

  • Which format fits the audience: a data presentation, a facilitated discussion, a workshop, a written report?
  • Who belongs in the room: faculty first, or chairs and deans from the start?
  • What outcome do you want: shared understanding, a formal resolution, a committee recommendation?
  • Which follow-up structure sustains momentum after the event?

Format options by goal

  • To build internal consensus first: a faculty workshop using this Playbook, anonymized survey results returned to participants, or a program retreat with a data presentation.
  • To make the case to administration: a one-page data brief for the chair, a program review addendum, or a section of the accreditation self-study.
  • To create a shared record: a program assessment report, a faculty senate white paper, or a joint letter to the dean signed by multiple faculty.
  • To raise urgency: a direct conversation with the chair anchored in the CCCC standard, or a formal request to present data to the dean.

The one-page principle

  • Administrators often do not read long documents. Prepare one page: the current situation, the national standard, the gap, and one specific, bounded request.
  • Pair the page with a longer evidence document for readers who want the full case.

Where momentum exists, move through official channels. Bring a formal proposal to the curriculum committee, the faculty senate, or the relevant administrative body, framed with disciplinary authority and local data.

Where official channels are blocked, other routes remain. The Trojan Horse approach places your request inside an initiative the administration already values, such as accreditation preparation, student success, or strategic planning. The data stays the same and the frame changes.

Decision framework

  • The decision-maker is persuadable: prepare a formal proposal with data and go through official channels.
  • A governance body has authority: bring the issue to the curriculum committee, the faculty senate, or the relevant body.
  • Official channels are blocked: consider the options below.

When official channels are blocked

  • Trojan Horse: place the request inside an initiative the administration already values, such as accreditation preparation, a retention initiative, DEI planning, or a strategic planning cycle.
  • Faculty senate or governance: bring a resolution or a white paper. A non-binding resolution still creates a formal record and signals a collective faculty position.
  • Collective bargaining or union: where a union exists, contract negotiation and grievance processes provide formal recourse. Consult your union representative first.
  • Accreditor: regional accreditors such as SACSCOC and HLC review evidence of student learning and success. Frame your data in accreditation terms.
  • Professional organizations: TYCA, CWPA, MLA, and AAUP publish standards, and some offer consultation or statements of concern. Confirm what each currently offers. The status of CCCC changed in July 2026, and its statements remain available through the CCCC-L repository (opens in a new tab).
  • Public or press: a last resort, appropriate only for severe and well-documented violations.

Scenario Case Studies

Each scenario shows the six-step process at work in one institutional context. Filter by institution type and position to find the closest case.

Scenario 1 · Community college · Continuing-contract faculty · Course caps and workload

Course Caps and Workload After State Developmental Education Reforms

Moderate risk

A continuing-contract faculty member at a large open-access community college seeks to reduce first-year composition caps from 27 to 22 after the state eliminated non-degree-credit developmental writing. Under the new model all students, including those previously placed in developmental sections, enroll in standard first-year composition. The DFW rate has risen to 33 percent.

Recommended approach

Begin with data (Step 3). Work with institutional research to disaggregate DFW rates by placement pathway and by cap size, and test whether the increase tracks the removal of developmental support. Cite the TYCA workload white paper and the CCCC Principles as authority. Present the cap reduction as a student success measure tied to the institution's retention goals, and bring it to the curriculum committee.

Scenario 2 · Regional university · Tenure-track WPA · Curriculum under legislative restriction

Preserving Anti-Racist Pedagogy Under "Divisive Concepts" Legislation

Higher risk

A fifth-year tenure-track WPA works at a state institution under enacted "divisive concepts" legislation. The composition committee developed anti-racist approaches over three years, and the new law threatens to constrain syllabi and discussion. The dean has requested a "compliance review" without guidance on what compliance means.

Recommended approach

Seek legal clarity first: establish what the legislation prohibits, as distinct from what administrators assume it prohibits. Consult NCTE's intellectual freedom resources and the NCTE Position Statement on Supporting Teachers and Students in Discussing Complex Topics (2024). Build a faculty coalition before responding to the dean. Describe the pedagogy in terms of rhetorical competence and intellectual inquiry. Document everything in writing.

Scenario 3 · Comprehensive university · Tenured WPA · Early college on a compressed timeline

Resisting an Under-Resourced Early College Initiative

Lower risk

In late March a dean asks a tenured WPA to launch first-year writing in a new Early College program beginning in July. The WPA has concerns about instructor qualification, class size, curricular integrity, and student readiness. The dean is under pressure from the board to launch quickly.

Recommended approach

From a position of relative safety, answer "not yet" in place of "no." Anchor the response in the Joint Position Statement on Dual Enrollment in Composition (2019) and the Framework for Success in Postsecondary Writing (2011). Propose conditional agreement: qualified instructors, appropriate caps, a curriculum alignment process, and an adequate timeline. Put your concerns to the dean in writing. If the program proceeds without those conditions, the record protects you and shows good-faith effort.

Scenario 4 · Community college · Adjunct instructor · Online section parity

Online Section Caps Higher Than Face-to-Face Caps

Higher risk

An adjunct instructor discovers online first-year composition sections capped at 35 while face-to-face sections are capped at 27. Online instructors receive no differential pay and no reduced load, despite higher caps and different labor demands. No formal policy documents the difference.

Recommended approach

Collect data anonymously at first and survey other online instructors informally. Bring the data to a tenured ally who is willing to raise it through faculty governance. Cite the CCCC statement on online writing instruction (2013), which sets a cap of 20 for online writing courses with 15 preferred, and the TYCA workload white paper. Frame the issue as student equity: an instructor with 35 online students has less time for each student's writing than an instructor with 27 students in a classroom.

Scenario 5 · Research university · Assistant professor · Administrative work in tenure review

WPA Work Classified as Service Rather Than Scholarship

Moderate risk

A third-year tenure-track WPA learns through informal feedback of a problem in the tenure review: directing the first-year composition program is counting as service, despite three peer-reviewed articles about the program and national conference presentations. The pre-tenure review is six months away.

Recommended approach

Act before the pre-tenure review. Bring CWPA's Evaluating the Intellectual Work of Writing Administration (1998) and the CCCC guidelines on scholarship in rhetoric, writing, and composition (2018) to a meeting with your chair. Propose explicit language for the file: "program development scholarship" in place of "service." If the chair does not respond, take the same documents to the dean.

Scenario 6 · Liberal arts college · Tenured WPA · Rising adjunct ratio

75 Percent Adjunct-Taught Sections and Declining Program Coherence

Lower risk

Over five years the share of sections taught by adjunct faculty has grown from 40 to 75 percent through budget pressure and unreplaced retirements. The tenured WPA documents growing inconsistency in course design, falling student satisfaction scores, and rising DFW rates. The dean attributes the DFW increase to "student preparedness."

Recommended approach

You have the data and the position for a formal case. Cite the CCCC Statement on Working Conditions for Non-Tenure-Track Writing Faculty (2016), the TYCA workload white paper, and the CCCC statement on faculty hiring (2016). Present disaggregated data: DFW by instructor type, participation in faculty development by instructor type, and student satisfaction by section. Propose a specific intervention with costs. Bring it to the curriculum committee and the faculty senate at the same time.

Key Position Statements

The primary disciplinary authorities for labor and advocacy conversations. Bring printed copies to meetings.

CCCC statements are cited by title and date of adoption. Links lead to archived copies in the CCCC-L position statement repository (opens in a new tab). See the note on CCCC statements.

  • CCCC

    Principles for the Postsecondary Teaching of Writing

    The field's standard for class size (20 maximum, 15 preferred, 60 students per instructor per term) and for working conditions. Revised 2023. The first statement to cite in workload and cap discussions.

    Read the Principles (2023) (opens in a new tab)

  • CWPA

    Evaluating the Intellectual Work of Writing Administration

    Establishes writing program administration as scholarship for tenure and promotion review. Essential for pre-tenure WPAs arguing for appropriate evaluation of administrative work.

    Read the statement (1998) (opens in a new tab)

  • CCCC

    Statement on Working Conditions for Non-Tenure-Track Writing Faculty

    Addresses contract transparency, equitable pay, professional development, and workload for contingent faculty at every institution type.

    Read the statement (2016) (opens in a new tab)

  • TYCA

    White Paper on Two-Year College English Faculty Workload

    Reports a national survey of 1,062 two-year college English faculty on class size, teaching load, and labor conditions at open-access institutions.

    Read the white paper (2021) (opens in a new tab)

  • CCCC

    Scholarship in Rhetoric, Writing, and Composition: Guidelines for Faculty, Deans, and Chairs

    Guidelines for evaluating writing studies scholarship in tenure and promotion review, across institution types and ranks.

    Read the guidelines (2018) (opens in a new tab)

  • Joint statement

    Joint Position Statement on Dual Enrollment in Composition

    Sets requirements for dual enrollment and early college programs: instructor preparation, curriculum alignment, and an adequate timeline.

    Read the statement (2019) (opens in a new tab)

  • CCCC
